Examples

Well-known cases that were already verified by journalists, scientists or authorities.

FALSE

"COVID-19 vaccines contain microchips"

This rumor, which blamed Bill Gates, spread on social media during the pandemic. Vaccines do not contain microchips or tracking devices, and manufacturers and health agencies publish their ingredients.

How to spot it: no scientific source supports it, and it plays on fear.

FALSE

"Drinking bleach or disinfectant cures COVID-19"

This is false and dangerous. Health authorities warned that swallowing these products causes serious poisoning and does not protect against the virus.

How to spot it: miracle cures with no clinical studies are a red flag.

FALSE

"Pope Francis endorsed Donald Trump in 2016"

A website imitating a news outlet published this story during the U.S. election and it spread widely on Facebook. The Vatican made no such endorsement.

How to spot it: the site was unknown and no serious outlet reported it.

TRUE

"Current climate change is mainly caused by human activity"

This is the conclusion of the scientific consensus. Organizations such as the IPCC and NASA state that burning fossil fuels is the main cause of recent warming.

How to check it: many independent studies and scientific bodies support it.

TRUE

"Humans reached the Moon in 1969"

Apollo 11 landed on the Moon. Moon rocks have been studied by scientists in several countries, and laser reflectors left there are still used to measure the distance to the Moon.

How to check it: there is physical evidence that independent teams can verify.

MISLEADING

"The 1938 War of the Worlds radio broadcast caused mass panic"

Orson Welles' adaptation did air and some listeners were alarmed, but historians say the mass panic was exaggerated by newspapers of the time, which saw radio as a competitor.

How to spot it: a real event told with more drama than it had.
